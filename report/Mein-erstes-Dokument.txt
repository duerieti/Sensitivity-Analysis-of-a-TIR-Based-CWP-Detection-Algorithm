Chapter 1

Introduction
In recent years, thermal infrared (TIR) remote sensing from unmanned aerial vehicles
(UAVs) and other airborne platforms has become an established approach for mapping
thermal heterogeneity in river systems (Casas-Mulet et al., 2020; Dugdale, 2016; Kuhn
et al., 2021; Torgersen et al., 2001). Mapping this heterogeneity is ecologically important:
the cold-water patches it reveals can act as refugia that are essential for the survival
of thermally sensitive fish species, particularly as river temperatures in Switzerland are
expected to rise due to climate change. Despite this importance, there is currently no
comprehensive information about the presence, scale, and location of such cold-water
patches in Swiss water bodies (Tonolla and Antonetti, 2021, 2026).
As part of a cantonal project aiming to quantify the temperature heterogeneity and detect
cold-water patches in the Emme River and its tributaries, a helicopter-based survey was
conducted to collect TIR imagery covering over a 100 km river transect. These TIR images
were processed using a newly developed algorithm for the automatic detection of coldwater patches, extracting longitudinal and transversal temperature gradients alongside
the detected cold-water spots. Detailed information regarding the full workflow can be
found in (Tonolla and Antonetti, 2021, 2026); the present thesis builds directly on this
existing algorithm and the resulting dataset.
The CWP detection algorithm relies on externally configured parameters, and sensitivity
analyses performed in (Tonolla and Antonetti, 2026) indicate that the detection results
can vary significantly based on the chosen parameter values, with the step length and
the temperature delta parameters having a particularly strong influence. The algorithm
divides the river reach into fixed-length segments and computes a separate local reference
temperature for each segment; visual inspection of existing detection results revealed that
this can produce abrupt cut-off artefacts, where a cold-water patch spanning two adjacent
segments is compared against two different reference temperatures and truncated at the
segment boundary, even though the underlying thermal anomaly continues beyond it.
Because cold-water tributaries tend to produce elongated thermal anomalies extending
substantially along the river channel, such patches are disproportionately likely to span
a segment boundary, suggesting that step length sensitivity may be elevated specifically
for tributary-caused CWPs. A systematic and more extensive sensitivity analysis of the
algorithm is therefore needed to test this assumption, to better characterise the resulting
variance more broadly, and to inform potential algorithmic improvements. Performing
such an analysis requires executing the algorithm across a large number of parameter
combinations. The existing algorithm was found to require between 1.25 and 2 hours per

Research Questions
Based on the above, this thesis pursues three objectives: first, to investigate whether
the existing CWP detection algorithm can be reimplemented in a computationally more
efficient form while producing equivalent outputs; second, to systematically investigate
the algorithm’s sensitivity to its parameters, with particular focus on how the presence
of tributary inflows interacts with this sensitivity; and third, to derive adjustments to
the detection algorithm that could reduce this parameter sensitivity while maintaining
ecologically plausible detection results. These objectives are formalised in the following
three research questions:
Research question 1 Can the existing CWP detection algorithm be reimplemented in
a computationally more efficient form, while producing outputs that are equivalent
to those of the original implementation?
Research question 2 How does the step length parameter contribute to the variance
of the detected CWP area for tributary-caused CWPs, compared to non-tributarycaused CWPs?
H0 The sensitivity of the detected CWP area to the step length parameter does not differ
between tributary-caused and non-tributary-caused CWPs.
H1 The sensitivity of the detected CWP area to the step length parameter is significantly
higher for tributary-caused CWPs than for non-tributary-caused CWPs.
Research question 3 Building on the findings of Tonolla and Antonetti (2026) sensitivity analysis: what adjustments could be made to the detection algorithm to reduce
variability with respect to its parameters while also achieving plausible detection
results?

Theoretical Background
2.1

Sensitivity Analysis

According to Saltelli et al. sensitivity analysis is “The study of how uncertainty in the
output of a model [. . . ] can be apportioned to different sources of uncertainty in the model
input” (Saltelli, 2004). This definition rests on a broad notion of what counts as a model
input: in sensitivity analysis, everything that causes variation in the output of the model
is classified as an input (Saltelli et al., 2007). Providing an example: if a linear regression
of a target variable Y is performed, then not only are the predictors X1 , . . . , Xn considered
as model inputs, but also the parameters θ1 , . . . , θn that correspond to the predictors. So
the model:
Y = f (X1 , X2 , θ1 , θ2 )

(1-1)

would have four model inputs from the view of sensitivity analysis.
Sensitivity analysis provides a range of benefits. Firstly it allows the modeller to apply
a strategy called “Factor Fixing”: if the model output Y is found to be independent of
one or multiple inputs Xi , then these can be set to fixed constants, making sensitivity
analysis a tool for model simplification (Saltelli et al., 2007). Secondly, sensitivity analysis
generally improves model validity by identifying highly influential and therefore critical
model components (Ligmann-Zielinska, 2013).

2.2

The Morris Method

The Morris method belongs to the group of screening methods in sensitivity analysis.
Screening methods require only a small number of model evaluations, making them computationally cheap and therefore well suited to models that are expensive to run (Campolongo
et al., 2007; Saltelli et al., 2007). The resulting sensitivity indices cannot be interpreted
quantitatively, but they do allow for a qualitative, ordinal ranking of which model inputs
are more important than others (Campolongo et al., 2011). As a global method, Morris
explores the entire input space, meaning that sensitivity scores reflect average behaviour
across the full parameter space rather than being tied to a single point (Saltelli and Annoni, 2010). The following explanation of the Morris method closely follows (Saltelli et al.,
2007) and is explained more explicitly in chapter 3 of their book.

Elementary Effects and the Sampling Grid

At the core of the Morris method sits a k-dimensional model Y with inputs X1 , . . . , Xk .
The model inputs are defined in the k-dimensional unit cube, meaning that Xi can take
on a value in the interval [0, 1]. In the Morris method, this input space is discretized into
a grid Ω, which is has p levels and is used as a basis for the computation of so-called
elementary effects. An elementary effect of the ith input parameter Xi is defined as:
EEi =

Y (X1 , X2 , . . . , Xi−1 , Xi + ∆, . . . , Xk ) − Y (X1 , X2 , . . . , Xk )
∆

(2-2)

Where ∆ represents some step along the grid Ω. Figure 2-1, taken from (Saltelli et al.,
2007), shows an example of such a grid for a two-parameter, four-level case.

Fig. 2-1: An example grid Ω. The grid features two parameters X1 , X2 , meaning that it is a
two-dimensional grid. Further, it features four levels (p = 4). The step ∆ chosen here
is 23 and indicated by the arrows.

The Morris method occupies itself with finding the statistical distribution of each of the
i elementary effects. Fi is thereby used to denote the distribution of EEi , in other words
Fi ∼ EEi (Saltelli et al., 2007).

2.2.2

Trajectory-Based Sampling

To populate Fi with multiple samples of EEi , the Morris method uses an efficient trajectorybased sampling design that obtains as many elementary effects EEi from as few model
evaluations as possible. Figure 2-2, taken from (Saltelli et al., 2007), shows an example
trajectory through a three-dimensional sampling grid.

10

Sensitivity Analysis of CWP

Etienne Dürig AD 23

Fig. 2-2: An example Morris trajectory through a three-dimensional sampling grid (k = 3, p = 6).
The trajectory consists of k + 1 = 4 points.

From this figure it becomes visible that a trajectory consists of k+1 points x(1) , x(2) , . . . , x(k+1) ,
where k is the dimension of the sampling grid. Each point differs from its predecessor by a
step of ∆ in exactly one grid direction, so that consecutive points differ in only one model
input Xi . The trajectory is constructed such that each dimension of the grid is stepped
along exactly once. Each trajectory yields exactly k elementary effects EEi – one for each
parameter spanning the sampling grid – and requires k + 1 model evaluations.
To build up Fi for every factor, r such trajectories are generated. Since each trajectory
contributes exactly one elementary effect per factor, each Fi ends up containing r samples.
The total number of model evaluations required therefore is:
N = r · (k + 1)

(2-3)

(Saltelli et al., 2007).

2.2.3

Choosing ∆ and r

The choice of r and ∆ is not independent of p. When p is chosen to be even, the choice ∆ =
p/2
p−1 is ideal, as it has the additional property of guaranteeing equal-probability sampling
from the grid, meaning that all grid levels are sampled with equal probability across
the r trajectories (Saltelli et al., 2007). There is no clear rule regarding the number of
trajectories r to pick. Generally, as the number of levels p in the grid increases, the number
of trajectories r also needs to increase. Otherwise, increasing p serves little purpose, as
the grid will not be sufficiently explored to feature all levels if the number of trajectories
is too small (Saltelli et al., 2007). Generally, the number of trajectories is usually chosen
somewhere between 10–50 (Campolongo et al., 2007).

Sensitivity Measures: µ, σ, and µ∗

Once the distributions Fi have been mapped out, the mean µi and the standard deviation
σi of Fi are computed, which are the final sensitivity indices of the morris method. These
two statistical measures together provide insight into the influence of the ith parameter
Xi – which corresponds to the ith elementary effect EEi – onto the model:
If µi deviates strongly from 0, then this means that the elementary effect EEi generally
takes on non-zero values and therefore that the model output changes notably when a
step of ∆ is performed along the dimension spanned by Xi . This ultimately means that
changes in Xi generally affect the model output Y considerably, and that Xi therefore
receives a high sensitivity score.
If σi is large, this means that the distribution Fi is spread out considerably over a larger
range. This indicates that the computed elementary effects EEi generally differed depending on their location in the grid. This behaviour points either to nonlinearity regarding Xi
or to the fact that Xi interacts with other input parameters. Which of the two effects is
responsible for the high standard deviation – or whether both contribute simultaneously –
cannot be determined, which is a shortcoming of the Morris method (Saltelli et al., 2007).
Figure 2-3 provides a visual example of possible F along with the associated µ and σ
values and their corresponding interpretation.

Fig. 2-3: Four example distributions Fi of a parameter Xi , illustrating how µ and σ jointly
characterize parameter influence.

Situations can occur where Fi contains many extreme values, yet the distribution is symmetric around zero. One such example is visualized in the upper-left plot of Figure 2-3.
In such cases, computing the mean value µi yields a value close to zero, leading to the
assumption that the parameter Xi is noninfluential. This would be a faulty assumption,
as the sampled elementary effects EEi are substantial in magnitude but just cancel each

other out. This phenomenon can be detected by always taking σi into consideration,
since in this scenario σi is large. It is, however, more common to additionally sample a
distribution Gi , which captures the magnitude of the elementary effects |EEi |. We then
compute the mean of this distribution, µ∗i , which is another commonly used sensitivity
index and can be interpreted like µi . Since it only captures magnitude, Gi however never
features negative values, and cancellation effects can therefore not occur (Saltelli et al.,
2007). Figure 2-4 provides a visual example of this mechanism.

Fig. 2-4: The difference of µ and µ⋆ visualized with their underlying distribution Fi and Gi . While
the µ lies at around zero, µ⋆ significantly deviates from zero.

2.3

Sensitivity analysis for Spatial Models

The Morris method, like most global sensitivity analysis methods, is defined for models
with a scalar output: a single value Y is required for each model evaluation (Saltelli et al.,
2007). The CWP detection algorithm, however, produces a spatially distributed output.
Applying the Morris method to such a model therefore requires a spatial aggregation step
to transform the spatial output to a scalar value, e.g. using a statistical aggregation
function such as the mean (Lilburne and Tarantola, 2009).

2.4

Coldwater Patches

Cold water patches (CWPs) are areas in rivers and streams where the water temperature
is measurably colder than that of the main channel. The temperature difference used to
define a CWP varies across the literature but is typically set between 0.5 and 3 degrees
Celsius below the main channel temperature (Casas-Mulet et al., 2020; Wawrzyniak et al.,
2016). Cold water patches are not automatically cold water refugia. Cold water refugia
are cold water patches that are actively used by cold-water organisms for thermal relief
(Mejia et al., 2023). The ecological importance of cold water refugia is expected to increase
13

Sensitivity Analysis of CWP

as river temperatures rise due to climate change (Kurylyk et al., 2015). CWPs can arise
from a range of physical mechanisms. A common cause is the inflow of a colder tributary-.
Other generating mechanisms include groundwater upwelling, lateral seeps or hyporheic
exchange (Mejia et al., 2023).

Methods
This chapter describes the operationalisation of the three research questions into a set
of methodological steps, which are then described in detail. The first section addresses
research question 1 by describing the reimplementation of the CWP detection algorithm
and the comparison of its outputs and runtime against the original. The second section addresses research question 2 by describing the Morris sensitivity analysis, including
the selection and classification of CWPs, the computation of sensitivity indices, and the
subsequent visualisation and statistical modelling. The third section addresses research
question 3 by describing the algorithm modifications derived from the sensitivity analysis
findings. A description of the data and study area is provided first.

3.1

Data and Study Area

The Emme river originates in an extensive moorland between the Hohgant and Augstmatthorn massifs and flows through the Emmental region before joining the Aare near
Zuchwil/Luterbach. The catchment covers approximately 974 km2 with a mean elevation
of 845 m a.s.l., and land cover is dominated by agricultural land (52.1%) and forested
areas (37.9%). During summer thunderstorms, the river can rise rapidly within minutes
and transport large quantities of driftwood; as a consequence, the lower reaches have been
heavily engineered (Tonolla and Antonetti, 2026).
Tonolla and Antonetti (2026) acquired high-resolution TIR and RGB orthophotos along
a 69 km transect of the Emme main channel, spanning from Kemmeribodenbad BE to
Biberist SO, as well as imagery of three tributary rivers: the Ilfis, Grüene, and Urtene.
The TIR imagery was collected from a helicopter platform using a FLIR SC655 thermal
infrared camera. In this thesis, only the TIR and RGB orthophotos of the 69 km main
channel section are used. The TIR orthophotos serve as the primary input to the CWP
detection algorithms, while the RGB orthophotos are used in a downsampled state as
background images for selected visualisations and were not used for any further analytical
purpose. The 69 km river transect is covered by three partially overlapping single-band
TIR orthophotos. Figure 1-1 provides an overview of the study area while Table 1-1
summarises their technical specifications of the TIR ortophotos.

3.2

Algorithm Reimplementation

The Morris sensitivity analysis conducted in this thesis required executing the CWP detection algorithm across hundreds of parameter combinations, each applied to all three
TIR orthophotos from Table 1-1, amounting to over 1000 individual algorithm runs in
total. Completing this within a feasible timespan required both a speed-optimized reimplementation and parallel execution on ZHAW’s High Performance Cluster (HPC). All
benchmarking was performed directly on the cluster to ensure that optimisation gains
carry over to the HPC infrastructure.

3.2.1

GDAL-Based Reimplementation

The reworked algorithm (v2) preserves the computational logic of the original implementation (v1) of Tonolla and Antonetti (2026) but differs fundamentally in how each step is
mechanistically executed. Where v1 processes the raster slab-by-slab in memory using R
loops and terra/sf functions, v2 replaces these with calls to the GDAL command line
interface, operating directly on raster and vector files on disk rather than holding intermediate results in memory. Table 2-2 gives a high-level overview of v2; the tool or function
used in each step is listed in the right-hand column.

3.4

Algorithm Modification

Two findings from Tonolla and Antonetti (2026) informed specific algorithmic decisions
in the design of v3. First, the buffer pixel count was found to have little influence on
the detected CWP area, qualifying it as a candidate for factor fixing – it was therefore
internalized in v3 and set to a constant value of 3. Second, the step length parameter was
identified as having a non-negligible influence on the detected CWP area, motivating a
fundamental redesign of the reference temperature computation. The temperature delta
parameter is retained as a free parameter, as different thresholds are used in the literature
to define CWPs and the user must therefore retain the ability to set it according to their
chosen definition.

3.4.1

Reference Temperature Redesign

In v1 and v2, the reference temperature for each pixel is determined by the median temperature of the slab segment to which that pixel belongs. This approach has two consequences:
it requires a user-defined step length, and it produces a reference temperature field that
is piecewise constant – uniform within each slab and discontinuous at slab boundaries.
CWPs that happen to span a slab boundary are therefore compared against two different
reference temperatures, which can produce artificial cutoffs in the detected patch extent.
In v3, this is replaced by an inverse distance weighting (IDW) approach. Rather than
dividing the reach into fixed slabs, reference temperature sample points are obtained by
sampling multiple step length values from a uniform distribution and extracting the median temperature at each resulting segment’s visual center. These sample points, pooled
across all sampled step lengths, are then used to compute a spatially continuous reference
temperature raster via IDW, where each cell’s reference temperature is a distance-weighted
average of the surrounding sample point temperatures. This guarantees that the reference
temperature is a spatially continuous field, and that no sharp cutoffs at slab boundaries
can occur.

5.1.1

Speed Comparison

The optimized algorithm (v2) shows substantially shorter execution times than v1 across

regardless of step length, while v1’s execution time increases as step length decreases,
ranging from approximately 1.25 hours at 1200 m to approximately 2 hours at 400 m. This
difference in scaling behaviour is consistent with how the two implementations process the
river reach. v2 is loop-free: GDAL operates on the full raster in a single pass, regardless
of the number of segments. v1 processes the reach one slab at a time using R loops, each
iteration incurring a fixed overhead independent of slab size. At shorter step lengths the
reach is divided into more slabs, shifting the overhead-to-useful-work ratio unfavourably
and increasing total execution time. This mechanistic account is a hypothesis and was not
formally tested.

5.1.2

Difference in CWP Polygons between v1 and v2

The CWP polygons produced by v1 and v2 are in general agreement with respect to location and spatial extent, as reflected by Jaccard similarities of 0.936, 0.956, and 0.976
across the three validation runs. The observed discrepancies are confined to the pixel level
– typically affecting a small number of pixels at patch boundaries – and no systematic
spatial bias is apparent. The most likely source of these discrepancies is the difference
in how terra::extract() and exactextractr::exact_extract() handle partially covered pixels: the 50% coverage-fraction threshold used to approximate terra’s centre-point
inclusion rule is not exactly equivalent, and small differences in extracted reference temperatures propagate to pixel-level differences in the binary CWP raster. The high Jaccard
similarities, combined with the observation that the discrepancies can be attributed to
a minor extraction artefact rather than a fundamental algorithmic difference, support

5.2

Sensitivity Analysis

This section interprets the Morris sensitivity analysis results, first addressing the specific hypothesis regarding step-length sensitivity in tributary-caused versus non-tributarycaused CWP, and then discussing the overall parameter sensitivity ranking across all three
varied parameters.

5.2.1

Step Length Sensitivity by CWP Class

The boxplot comparison of µ∗ values between tributary-caused and non-tributary-caused
CWP, shown in Figure 2-3, shows a tendency consistent with H1: tributary-caused CWP
display a wider spread in step-length sensitivity, with a higher third quartile and higher
maximum values than the non-tributary-caused group. However, a closer inspection reveals that this pattern is not uniform within the tributary-caused class – some tributarycaused CWP show sensitivity values comparable to the non-tributary-caused group, while
others are substantially more sensitive. This within-class heterogeneity suggests that tributary presence alone is not a sufficient condition for high sensitivity to the step length
parameter.
The scatter plot in Figure 2-5 and the linear model together point to CWP area as the
more proximate driver of step-length sensitivity. CWPs with larger mean area tend to
show higher µ∗ values, and this relationship is approximately linear. The linear model
confirms this: the mean area predictor is highly significant (p < 0.001) with a positive
slope, while the mean temperature delta predictor is not significant (p = 0.316).
A mechanistic explanation for the area–sensitivity relationship follows from the structure
of the algorithm. CWPs in a river system tend to be elongated rather than wide, since
the river channel constrains lateral extent while longitudinal extent is less bounded. Large
CWP are therefore predominantly long CWP. A long CWP has a higher probability of
spanning the boundary between two adjacent slabs, and when this occurs, the two parts
of the patch are compared against different reference temperatures – one computed from
each slab’s reference strip. If those reference temperatures differ (which is more likely
near a tributary inflow, where sharp longitudinal temperature gradients exist), the flagging threshold is effectively applied inconsistently across the patch, introducing variability
in the detected CWP extent that is directly attributable to the step length parameter.
Smaller and therefore shorter CWP are more likely to fall entirely within a single slab,
and are therefore less exposed to this effect.
Tributary inflows are therefore best understood not as a direct cause of step-length sensitivity, but as one mechanism that can produce large, elongated CWP by introducing
substantial volumes of cold water that generate extended thermal anomalies along the
river. In the present dataset, large CWP occur exclusively in the tributary-caused class,
which explains the elevated sensitivity observed in that group. Whether equally large
non-tributary-caused CWP would show comparable sensitivity cannot be determined, as
no such patches are present in the dataset.

5.2.2

Overall Sensitivity across all Parameters

The overall µ∗ distributions across all 24 annotated locations, shown in Figure 2-4, reveal
a clear ranking of parameter influence with respect to detected CWP area. The buffer
pixel count is consistently non-influential, with µ∗ values close to zero across all locations.
The corresponding σ values are similarly low, suggesting that the elementary effects of
buffer pixel count are both small in magnitude and consistent across the parameter space.
This indicates that there neither is a strong main effect nor any meaningful interactions
with the other parameters. This is consistent with Tonolla and Antonetti (2026), who
likewise identify the buffer pixel count as non-influential in their sensitivity analysis of the
CWP detection algorithm.
The temperature delta parameter shows the highest µ∗ values overall, indicating that it
is the most influential of the three parameters with respect to CWP area which is again
consistent with Tonolla and Antonetti (2026). The elevated σ values for temperature
delta suggest that its elementary effects vary across the parameter space, pointing to the
presence of nonlinearity or interactions with the other parameters. The former is rather
plausible: the relationship between the flagging threshold and the detected CWP area is
unlikely to be linear. Consider a thermal anomaly whose mean temperature falls just 0.1°C
below the current detection threshold – the entirety of that anomaly’s area is undetected.
Increasing temperature delta by 0.1°C brings that entire area above the threshold in a
single step, producing a large change in detected area from a small parameter change.
The same increment applied elsewhere in the parameter space may flag no new area at
all.
The step length parameter occupies an intermediate position, with µ∗ values clearly above
zero but consistently below those of temperature delta. Like temperature delta, step
length also shows elevated σ values, suggesting interactions with the other parameters
or nonlinear behaviour. Here the results again agree with Tonolla and Antonetti (2026),
which identified the step length parameter as beeing of intermediate influence in their
sensitivity analysis.

Algorithm Modification

The replication of the buffer pixel count and temperature delta findings from Tonolla and
Antonetti (2026) within the present work, using an independently implemented sensitivity
analysis lends additional credibility to both sets of results and suggests that these sensitivity patterns are robust properties of the CWP detection algorithm rather than artefacts of
a particular study context. This agreement between the two analyses provides a stronger
basis for the design decisions underlying v3 than either analysis alone would afford.
The substantially lower Jaccard similarities obtained for v3 against v1 (0.325, 0.334, and
0.287) are expected given the fundamental redesign of the reference temperature step, and
do not in themselves indicate that v3 performs worse than v1. The difference between the
two algorithms at least partly originates from improved behaviour. The slab-boundary
truncation artefact identified as a direct consequence of step-length sensitivity no longer
occurs in v3, producing CWP detections that extend naturally rather than ending abruptly
at segment boundaries. Similarly, CWPs that were artificially severed into multiple fragments by a slab boundary in v1 are detected as single contiguous patches in v3, which is
likely a more ecologically accurate representation of the underlying thermal anomaly.
What remains less certain are the new CWP detections present in v3 but entirely absent
in v1. A plausible explanation is that these represent genuine CWPs that were fragmented
below the minimum area threshold by a slab boundary in v1 and therefore filtered out.
However, as visible in Figure 3-6c, at least one such detection is large enough that slap
boundary fragmentation alone cannot account for its complete absence in v1. Therefore, an
alternative explanation would be required for cases of this size. Determining whether these
additional detections represent real cold-water patches would require in-situ assessment of
river morphology and temperature.

5.4

Future Improvements

The following subsections identify three methodological limitations of the present work
and outline directions in which future studies could address them.

5.4.1

Subjective Selection and Classification of CWP

Both the selection and classification of the CWP were carried out subjectively based on
visual inspection of the fuzzy overlay raster. This approach is prone to confirmation
bias on two levels. First, the judgment about variability and the judgment about class
membership were both made with the study hypothesis already in mind. CWPs that
were selected are therefore likely those that were most easily attributable to one of the
two classes – patches that clearly appeared variable and clearly appeared tributary-caused
or non-tributary-caused. More nuanced cases, where variability was ambiguous or class
attribution uncertain, were left out. The resulting dataset is consequently biased towards
extreme and obvious cases, and may not be representative of the broader population of
CWPs in the Emme system. Second, given that H1 of research question 2 was considered
plausible prior to the analysis based on prior observations, the selection process may have
been further skewed towards patches that would support H1, even if this influence was
not conscious.

Two methodological improvements could reduce these limitations in future work. First,
the variability assessment and CWP selection could be replaced by a fully algorithmic approach, removing human judgment from the process. Caution is however warranted: upon
defining the rules of a process-based algorithm, the analyst may inadvertently encode the
same biases that affect manual selection. Similarly, a data-driven approach would need
to learn from a ground truth dataset that is itself produced by human labeling, meaning
the bias present in the labeling process would be inherited by the model. Second, if manual classification cannot be avoided, inter-rater reliability assessment should be included:
multiple independent observers, each blind to the study hypothesis, could classify the
same set of candidate CWPs, and the level of agreement between raters – quantified for
example using Cohen’s κ – would provide an empirical measure of classification reliability.
Such a dataset could further serve as ground truth for training a data-driven classification algorithm, combining the transparency of manual labeling with the scalability of an
automated approach.

5.4.2

Sample Size

With only n = 24 annotated CWP locations, the dataset is small by the standards of
statistical inference. While the significant area–sensitivity relationship detected by the
linear model suggests that the effect is strong enough to be visible at this sample size,
the risk of chance findings cannot be excluded. A larger sample would increase statistical
power and reduce the probability that the observed relationships are artefacts of the
specific locations selected.

5.4.3

Improvements in the v3 detection algorithm

Upon closer inspection, v3 introduces a parameter that was not explicitly treated as a
free parameter during the development of the algorithm: the decay power of the inverse
distance weighting scheme, which governs how quickly the influence of a reference point
diminishes with distance. In the current implementation this value is fixed at 2, which
is a common default, but the choice is arbitrary and its influence on the detected CWP
area has not been assessed. Strictly speaking, this means that the parameter reduction
achieved by v3 is incomplete: the decay power has effectively been fixed by convention
rather than by a principled sensitivity analysis. A dedicated sensitivity analysis of the
IDW decay power would be needed to either justify fixing it at its current value through
factor fixing, or to expose it as a free parameter that the user should be able to configure.
