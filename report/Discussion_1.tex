\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Algorithm Reimplementation}
\label{sec:rq1_discussion}

This section discusses the results of the output equivalence comparison 
and the runtime benchmarking between v1 and v2, and assesses whether 
the sensitivity analysis findings derived from v2 can be transferred 
to v1.

\subsection{Speed Comparison}

The optimized algorithm (v2) shows substantially shorter execution times 
than v1 across all three tested step lengths, with the performance 
advantage most pronounced at shorter step lengths. v2 maintains an 
approximately constant execution time of around 0.5 hours regardless of 
step length, while v1's execution time increases as step length decreases, 
ranging from approximately 1.25 hours at 1200~m to approximately 2 hours 
at 400~m. This difference in scaling behaviour is consistent with how the two 
implementations process the river reach. v2 is loop-free: GDAL operates 
on the full raster in a single pass, regardless of the number of segments. v1 processes the reach 
one slab at a time using R loops, each iteration incurring a fixed overhead 
independent of slab size. At shorter step lengths the reach is divided into 
more slabs, shifting the overhead-to-useful-work ratio unfavourably and 
increasing total execution time. This mechanistic account is a hypothesis 
and was not formally tested.


\subsection{Difference in CWP Polygons between v1 and v2}

The CWP polygons produced by v1 and v2 are in general agreement with 
respect to location and spatial extent, as reflected by Jaccard 
similarities of 0.936, 0.956, and 0.976 across the three validation 
runs. The observed discrepancies are confined to the pixel level -- 
typically affecting a small number of pixels at patch boundaries -- 
and no systematic spatial bias is apparent. The most likely source of these discrepancies is the difference in how 
\texttt{terra::extract()} and \texttt{exactextractr::exact\_extract()} 
handle partially covered pixels: the 50\% coverage-fraction threshold 
used to approximate \texttt{terra}'s centre-point inclusion rule is not 
exactly equivalent, and small differences in extracted reference 
temperatures propagate to pixel-level differences in the binary CWP 
raster. The high Jaccard similarities, combined with the observation that the 
discrepancies can be attributed to a minor extraction artefact rather 
than a fundamental algorithmic difference, support the conclusion that 
v2 replicates the behaviour of v1 to a degree sufficient to transfer 
sensitivity findings.


\section{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:rq2_discussion}

This section interprets the Morris sensitivity analysis results, first 
addressing the specific hypothesis regarding step-length sensitivity in 
tributary-caused versus non-tributary-caused CWP, and then discussing 
the overall parameter sensitivity ranking across all three varied parameters.

\subsection{Step Length Sensitivity by CWP Class}

The boxplot comparison of $\mu^*$ values between tributary-caused and 
non-tributary-caused CWP, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:step_length_sensitivity}, 
shows a tendency consistent with H1: tributary-caused CWP display a wider 
spread in step-length sensitivity, with a higher third quartile and higher 
maximum values than the non-tributary-caused group. However, a closer 
inspection reveals that this pattern is not uniform within the tributary-caused 
class -- some tributary-caused CWP show sensitivity values comparable to the 
non-tributary-caused group, while others are substantially more sensitive. This 
within-class heterogeneity suggests that tributary presence alone is not a 
sufficient condition for high sensitivity to the step length parameter.

The scatter plot in Figure~\ref{fig:mu_star_vs_area} and the linear model 
together point to CWP area as the more proximate driver of step-length 
sensitivity. CWPs with larger mean area tend to show higher $\mu^*$ values, 
and this relationship is approximately linear. The linear model confirms this: 
the mean area predictor is highly significant ($p < 0.001$) with a positive 
slope, while the mean temperature delta predictor is not significant 
($p = 0.316$).

A mechanistic explanation for the area--sensitivity relationship follows 
from the structure of the algorithm. CWPs in a river system tend to 
be elongated rather than wide, since the river channel constrains lateral extent 
while longitudinal extent is less bounded. Large CWP are therefore predominantly 
long CWP. A long CWP has a higher probability of spanning the boundary between 
two adjacent slabs, and when this occurs, the two parts of the patch are compared 
against different reference temperatures -- one computed from each slab's 
reference strip. If those reference temperatures differ (which is more likely 
near a tributary inflow, where sharp longitudinal temperature gradients exist), 
the flagging threshold is effectively applied inconsistently across the patch, 
introducing variability in the detected CWP extent that is directly attributable 
to the step length parameter. Smaller and therefore shorter CWP are more likely to fall 
entirely within a single slab, and are therefore less exposed to this effect.

Tributary inflows are therefore best understood not as a direct cause of 
step-length sensitivity, but as one mechanism that can produce large, elongated 
CWP by introducing substantial volumes of cold water that generate extended 
thermal anomalies along the river. In the present dataset, large CWP occur 
exclusively in the tributary-caused class, which explains the elevated 
sensitivity observed in that group. Whether equally large non-tributary-caused 
CWP would show comparable sensitivity cannot be determined, as no such patches 
are present in the dataset.

¨Neither H0 nor H1 is fully supported by the data. H0 is too strong a rejection 
as tributary presence is associated with a tendency towards higher step-length 
sensitivity. However, H1 overstates the role of tributary presence as a direct 
determinant of sensitivity. The more proximate driver appears to be CWP size, 
with tributary presence acting primarily as a driver of patch size rather than 
sensitivity directly. With no comparably large non-tributary-caused CWP present 
in the dataset, concluding that tributary-caused CWP are inherently more 
susceptible to the step length parameter is not fully justified as it is equally 
plausible that large non-tributary-caused CWP would show comparable sensitivity.


Interestingly, CWP area and mean temperature delta appear to be largely 
unrelated in the data, despite the intuitive expectation that a colder 
tributary might produce a larger thermal anomaly. This could suggest that 
tributary discharge plays a more important role than temperature difference 
in determining CWP size. A large, moderately cold tributary may produce 
a larger CWP than a small but very cold one. Discharge data were not 
collected during the survey, but could be a valuable addition in future work.

\subsection{Overall Sensitivity across all Parameters}


The overall $\mu^*$ distributions across all 24 annotated locations, shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:mu_star_overall}, reveal a clear ranking of parameter influence 
with respect to detected CWP area. The buffer pixel count is consistently 
non-influential, with $\mu^*$ values close to zero across all locations. The 
corresponding $\sigma$ values are similarly low, suggesting that the elementary 
effects of buffer pixel count are both small in magnitude and consistent across 
the parameter space. This indicates that there neither is a strong main effect nor any meaningful interactions with the other parameters. This is consistent with 
\citet{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026}, who likewise identify the buffer 
pixel count as non-influential in their sensitivity analysis of the CWP detection 
algorithm.

The temperature delta parameter shows the highest $\mu^*$ values overall, 
indicating that it is the most influential of the three parameters with respect 
to CWP area which is again consistent with 
\citet{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026}. The elevated $\sigma$ values for 
temperature delta suggest that its elementary effects vary across the parameter 
space, pointing to the presence of nonlinearity or interactions with the other 
parameters. The former is rather plausible: the relationship between the flagging 
threshold and the detected CWP area is unlikely to be linear. Consider a thermal 
anomaly whose mean temperature falls just 0.1\textdegree C below the current 
detection threshold -- the entirety of that anomaly's area is undetected. 
Increasing temperature delta by 0.1\textdegree C brings that entire area above 
the threshold in a single step, producing a large change in detected area from 
a small parameter change. The same increment applied elsewhere in the parameter 
space may flag no new area at all.

The step length parameter occupies an intermediate position, with $\mu^*$ values 
clearly above zero but consistently below those of temperature delta. Like 
temperature delta, step length also shows elevated $\sigma$ values, suggesting 
interactions with the other parameters or nonlinear behaviour. Here the results again agree with \cite{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026}, which identified the step length parameter as beeing of intermediate influence in their sensitivity analysis.

\section{Algorithm Modification}
\label{sec:rq3_discussion}

The replication of the buffer pixel count and temperature delta findings from 
\citet{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026} within the present work, using an 
independently implemented sensitivity analysis lends 
additional credibility to both sets of results and suggests that these sensitivity 
patterns are robust properties of the CWP detection algorithm rather than 
artefacts of a particular study context. This agreement between the two analyses 
provides a stronger basis for the design decisions underlying v3 than either 
analysis alone would afford.


The substantially lower Jaccard similarities obtained for v3 against v1 (0.325, 
0.334, and 0.287) are expected given the fundamental redesign of the reference 
temperature step, and do not in themselves indicate that v3 performs worse than 
v1. The difference between the two algorithms at least 
partly originates from improved behaviour. The slab-boundary truncation 
artefact identified as a direct consequence of step-length sensitivity no longer 
occurs in v3, producing CWP detections that extend naturally rather than ending 
abruptly at segment boundaries. Similarly, CWPs that were artificially severed 
into multiple fragments by a slab boundary in v1 are detected as 
single contiguous patches in v3, which is likely a more ecologically accurate 
representation of the underlying thermal anomaly.

What remains less certain are the new CWP detections present in v3 but entirely 
absent in v1. A plausible explanation is that these represent genuine CWPs 
that were fragmented below the minimum area threshold by a slab boundary in v1 
and therefore filtered out. However, as visible in Figure~\ref{fig:v3_new}, at 
least one such detection is large enough that slap boundary fragmentation alone 
cannot account for its complete absence in v1. Therefore, an alternative explanation 
would be required for cases of this size. Determining whether these additional 
detections represent real cold-water patches would require in-situ
assessment of river morphology and temperature.

\section{Future Improvements}

The following subsections identify three methodological limitations of the 
present work and outline directions in which future studies could address them.

\subsection{Subjective Selection and Classification of CWP}

Both the selection and classification of the CWP were carried out subjectively 
based on visual inspection of the fuzzy overlay raster. This approach is prone 
to confirmation bias on two levels. First, the judgment about variability and 
the judgment about class membership were both made with the study hypothesis 
already in mind. CWPs that were selected are therefore likely those that were 
most easily attributable to one of the two classes -- patches that clearly 
appeared variable and clearly appeared tributary-caused or non-tributary-caused. 
More nuanced cases, where variability was ambiguous or class attribution 
uncertain, were left out. The resulting dataset is consequently biased towards 
extreme and obvious cases, and may not be representative of the broader 
population of CWPs in the Emme system. Second, given that H1 of research 
question 2 was considered plausible prior to the analysis based on prior 
observations, the selection process may have been further skewed towards patches 
that would support H1, even if this influence was not conscious.

Two methodological improvements could reduce these limitations in future work. 
First, the variability assessment and CWP selection could be replaced by a fully 
algorithmic approach, removing human judgment from the process. Caution is 
however warranted: upon defining the rules of a process-based algorithm, the 
analyst may inadvertently encode the same biases that affect manual selection. 
Similarly, a data-driven approach would need to learn from a ground truth dataset 
that is itself produced by human labeling, meaning the bias present in the 
labeling process would be inherited by the model. Second, if manual 
classification cannot be avoided, inter-rater reliability assessment should be 
included: multiple independent observers, each blind to the study hypothesis, 
could classify the same set of candidate CWPs, and the level of agreement between 
raters -- quantified for example using Cohen's $\kappa$ -- would provide an 
empirical measure of classification reliability. Such a dataset could further 
serve as ground truth for training a data-driven classification algorithm, 
combining the transparency of manual labeling with the scalability of an 
automated approach.

\subsection{Sample Size}

With only $n = 24$ annotated CWP locations, the dataset is small by the 
standards of statistical inference. While the significant area--sensitivity 
relationship detected by the linear model suggests that the effect is strong 
enough to be visible at this sample size, the risk of chance findings cannot 
be excluded. A larger sample would increase statistical power and reduce the 
probability that the observed relationships are artefacts of the specific 
locations selected.

\subsection{Improvements in the v3 detection algorithm}

Upon closer inspection, v3 introduces a parameter that was not explicitly 
treated as a free parameter during the development of the algorithm: the decay 
power of the inverse distance weighting scheme, which governs how quickly the 
influence of a reference point diminishes with distance. In the current 
implementation this value is fixed at 2, which is a common default, but the 
choice is arbitrary and its influence on the detected CWP area has not been 
assessed. Strictly speaking, this means that the parameter reduction achieved 
by v3 is incomplete: the decay power has effectively been fixed by convention 
rather than by a principled sensitivity analysis. A dedicated sensitivity 
analysis of the IDW decay power would be needed to either justify fixing it at 
its current value through factor fixing, or to expose it as a free parameter 
that the user should be able to configure.
