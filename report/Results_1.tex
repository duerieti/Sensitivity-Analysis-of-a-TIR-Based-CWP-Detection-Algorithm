\section{Research Question 1: Algorithm Reimplementation}
\label{sec:rq1_results}

This section presents the results of the runtime benchmarking and output 
equivalence comparison between v1 and v2. The two subsections address the 
speed improvement achieved by the reimplementation and the degree to which 
the two implementations produce spatially consistent CWP detections.


\subsection{Runtime Comparison}

Figure~\ref{fig:speed_comparison} shows the execution time of v1 and v2 as a 
function of segment length. The execution time of v2 is approximately 0.5 hours 
across all three tested segment lengths, remaining largely constant. In contrast, 
v1 shows execution times ranging from approximately 2 hours at a segment length 
of 400~m to approximately 1.25 hours at 1200~m, with execution time increasing 
as segment length decreases (i.e., as the number of segments increases).

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Bilder/speed_comparison.png}
	\caption{Execution time of the original (v1) and optimized (v2) 
		implementations of \texttt{detect\_cwp\_single} as a function of segment 
		length. The optimized implementation shows execution times that are both 
		shorter and largely constant across segment lengths, whereas the original 
		implementation scales disproportionately with decreasing segment length 
		(i.e., increasing number of segments).}
	\label{fig:speed_comparison}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Output Equivalence}

The three comparison runs achieved Jaccard similarities of 0.936, 0.956, 
and 0.976, indicating generally good spatial agreement between v1 and v2. 
In all panels, v2 polygons are shown in red and v1 polygons in blue, 
overlaid at 50\% transparency. Figure~\ref{fig:v1v2_comparison} illustrates the range of agreement 
observed. Panel (a) shows a representative example of general agreement, 
with discrepancies confined to a small number of boundary pixels. Panel (b) 
shows a more pronounced boundary mismatch. Panel (c) shows an example of 
complete spatial agreement, where the two polygon sets are visually 
indistinguishable.

\section{Research Question 2: Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:rq2_results}

This section presents the Morris sensitivity analysis results across three 
complementary views: the sensitivity of detected CWP area to the step length 
parameter stratified by CWP class, the overall sensitivity ranking across all 
three varied parameters, and the relationship between CWP area and step-length 
sensitivity as quantified by the linear model.

\subsection{Sensitivity of CWP Area to Step Length}

Figure~\ref{fig:step_length_sensitivity} shows the Morris sensitivity indices 
$\mu^*$ and $\sigma$ with respect to the step length parameter, stratified by 
CWP class ($n = 10$ tributary-caused, $n = 14$ non-tributary-caused). All 
non-tributary-caused CWP show $\mu^*$ values below 0.2, indicating low 
sensitivity to the step length parameter across this class. The tributary-caused 
CWP show a wider spread in $\mu^*$: some tributary-caused CWP have sensitivity 
values comparable to the non-tributary-caused group, while others show 
substantially higher values of up to 0.3--0.6. The median $\mu^*$ values of the 
two classes are similar, with the primary difference lying in the upper tail of 
the tributary-caused distribution. The $\sigma$ values show a comparable pattern, 
with greater spread in the tributary-caused group.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Bilder/step_length_results.png}
	\caption{Morris sensitivity indices ($\mu^*$ and $\sigma$) for the 
		\texttt{step\_length} parameter with respect to cold water patch area, 
		stratified by patch class (red: tributary-caused, $n=10$; blue: 
		non-tributary-caused, $n=14$). Boxplots show the distribution across 
		bounding boxes.}
	\label{fig:step_length_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Overall Sensitivity across all Parameters}

Figure~\ref{fig:mu_star_overall} displays the $\mu^*$ values for all 24 CWP 
locations with respect to the three parameters varied in the second Morris 
experiment. The buffer pixel count shows consistently low $\mu^*$ values across 
all locations, with the entire distribution concentrated near zero. In contrast, 
both step length and temperature delta show distributions that deviate clearly 
from zero. The temperature delta parameter shows the highest $\mu^*$ values 
overall, suggesting it has the strongest influence on the detected CWP area 
across the set of annotated locations.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Bilder/mustar_sig_over_all.png}
	\caption{Overall $\mu^*$ sensitivity indices for \texttt{buffer\_px}, 
		\texttt{delta\_T}, and \texttt{step\_length} across all classified cold 
		water patches ($N = 24$ locations, $r = 64$ trajectories).}
	\label{fig:mu_star_overall}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Relationship between CWP Area and Step Length Sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:mu_star_vs_area} shows the 24 selected CWP along three 
dimensions simultaneously: mean CWP area averaged across all Morris runs 
(x-axis), $\mu^*$ with respect to the step length parameter (y-axis), mean 
temperature delta to the surrounding environment averaged across all Morris 
runs (color), and CWP class (shape).The data suggest a positive relationship 
between mean CWP area and sensitivity to the step length parameter: locations 
with larger mean CWP 
area tend to show higher $\mu^*$ values. No clear relationship between 
mean temperature delta and $\mu^*$ is visible from the colouring.

Table~\ref{tab:regression_mustar} shows the results of the linear model. 
The mean area predictor is highly significant ($p < 0.001$) with a positive 
slope, consistent with the visual pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:mu_star_vs_area}. 
The mean temperature delta predictor is not significant ($p = 0.556$). 
The model explains a substantial proportion of variance ($R^2 = 0.611$, 
Adjusted $R^2 = 0.572$).

\section{Research Question 3: Algorithm Modification}
\label{sec:rq3_results}

This section presents the evaluation of v3 relative to v1, covering both the 
quantitative Jaccard similarity comparison and the qualitative assessment of 
detection improvements resulting from the IDW-based reference temperature 
computation.

\subsection{Output Comparison of v3 against v1}

The CWP polygons produced by v3 were compared against those of v1 using the 
same Jaccard similarity metric applied in the output equivalence comparison of 
v1 and v2 (Section~\ref{sec:output_equivalence}). The Jaccard similarities 
obtained were 0.325, 0.334, and 0.287 for the three validation parameter 
combinations -- substantially lower than the values of 0.936, 0.956, and 0.976 
obtained for v2 versus v1. This indicates that v3 produces CWP detections that 
differ considerably from those of v1, which is expected: the IDW-based reference 
temperature computation represents a fundamental redesign of the core reference 
temperature step rather than a minor reimplementation, and is therefore not 
expected to replicate v1's outputs closely. The relevant question is not whether 
v3 agrees with v1, but whether v3's detections are ecologically more plausible.

Figure~\ref{fig:v3_qualitative} illustrates three qualitative improvements 
observed in v3 relative to v1. In each panel, v1 polygons and v3 polygons are 
overlaid with 50\% transparency, allowing areas of agreement and disagreement 
to be read directly from the overlap pattern.

	\centering
	\begin{subfigure}[t]{\textwidth}
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Bilder/v3_trunctuation_fix.png}
		\caption{Slab-boundary truncation eliminated. The v1 polygon ends 
			abruptly at the slab boundary; the v3 polygon extends naturally 
			beyond it.}
		\label{fig:v3_truncation}

Panel (a) shows the most direct improvement: in v1, the detected CWP extent is 
truncated abruptly at a slab boundary, not because the underlying thermal anomaly 
ends there, but because the reference temperature changes discontinuously at that 
point. In v3, the spatially continuous IDW reference temperature surface removes 
this artificial boundary, allowing the detected patch to extend naturally beyond 
the former boundary location and taper off gradually as the temperature difference 
to the surrounding river diminishes.

Panel (b) illustrates a direct consequence of eliminating slab-boundary 
truncations: CWPs previously reported as two separate fragments -- one on each 
side of a slab boundary -- are now detected as a single contiguous polygon, 
which is likely a more accurate representation of the underlying thermal anomaly.

Panel (c) shows an example of a CWP detected by v3 that is entirely absent in 
v1. A likely explanation is that the corresponding thermal anomaly straddles a 
slab boundary in v1, causing it to be split into two sub-threshold fragments 
that are subsequently removed by the minimum area filter. In v3, the absence of 
slab boundaries allows the anomaly to be detected as a single contiguous patch 
above the area threshold. Whether these additional detections represent genuine 
CWPs missed by v1, or false positives introduced by the IDW reference 
temperature surface, cannot be determined without independent ground-truth 
validation -- but the former interpretation is consistent with the mechanistic 
argument developed in Section~\ref{sec:rq3_methods}.
