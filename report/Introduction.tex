\chapter{Introduction}

In recent years, thermal infrared (TIR) remote sensing from unmanned aerial 
vehicles (UAVs) and other airborne platforms has become an established approach 
for mapping thermal heterogeneity in river systems
\citep{casas-mulet_unmanned_2020,kuhn_assessing_2021,torgersen_airborne_2001,
	dugdale_practitioners_2016}. Mapping this heterogeneity is ecologically important: 
the cold-water patches it reveals can act as refugia that are essential for the 
survival of thermally sensitive fish species, particularly as river temperatures 
in Switzerland are expected to rise due to climate change. 
Despite this importance, there is currently no comprehensive information about the 
presence, scale, and location of such cold-water patches in Swiss water bodies 
\citep{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026, tonolla_thermoscanning_2021}.

As part of a cantonal project aiming to quantify the temperature heterogeneity 
and detect cold-water patches in the Emme River and its tributaries, TIR imagery 
covering over a 100 km river transect were collected in a helicopter-based survey. 
These TIR images were then processed to extract longitudinal and transversal 
temperature gradients, as well as to detect cold-water spots. Here, 
a newly developed algorithm for the automatic detection of cold-water patches is 
deployed with the goal of mapping these patches. Detailed information regarding 
the whole workflow can be found in 
\citep{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026, tonolla_thermoscanning_2021}.

The CWP detection algorithm relies on externally configured parameters, and 
sensitivity analyses performed in \citep{tonolla_thermische-kartographie_2026} 
indicate that the detection results can vary significantly based on the chosen 
parameter values, with the step length and the temperature delta parameters having 
a particularly strong influence. A systematic and more extensive sensitivity 
analysis of the algorithm is therefore needed to better characterise this variance 
and to inform potential algorithmic improvements. Performing such an analysis 
requires executing the algorithm across a large number of parameter combinations. The existing algorithm was found to require 
between 1.25 and 2 hours per run on the available hardware, rendering a full 
sensitivity analysis infeasible without a faster implementation. A speed-optimized 
reimplementation is therefore a prerequisite for the sensitivity analysis itself.


\section*{Research Questions}

Based on the above, this thesis pursues three objectives: first, to investigate 
whether the existing CWP detection algorithm can be reimplemented in a 
computationally more efficient form while producing equivalent outputs; second, to 
systematically investigate the algorithm's sensitivity to its parameters, with 
particular focus on how the presence of tributary inflows interacts with this 
sensitivity; and third, to derive adjustments to the detection algorithm that could 
reduce this parameter sensitivity while maintaining ecologically plausible detection 
results. These objectives are formalised in the following three research questions:

\begin{description}
	\item[Research question 1] Can the existing CWP detection algorithm be 
	reimplemented in a computationally more efficient form, while producing 
	outputs that are equivalent to those of the original implementation?
	\item[Research question 2] How does the step length parameter contribute 
	to the variance of the detected CWP area for tributary-caused CWPs, 
	compared to non-tributary-caused CWPs? The algorithm defines a CWP by 
	comparing individual pixel temperatures against a local median reference 
	temperature calculated over a defined segment whose length is governed by 
	the step length parameter. Cold-water tributaries introduce sharp 
	longitudinal temperature gradients which can skew this calculation if the 
	step size is chosen sub-optimally. It is therefore hypothesised that 
	step length sensitivity is elevated near tributary inflows, giving rise to 
	the following hypotheses:
	\item[$H_0$] The sensitivity of the detected CWP area to the step length 
	parameter does not differ between tributary-caused and non-tributary-caused 
	CWPs.
	\item[$H_1$] The sensitivity of the detected CWP area to the step length 
	parameter is significantly higher for tributary-caused CWPs than for 
	non-tributary-caused CWPs.
	\item[Research question 3] Building on the findings from research questions 
	1 and 2: what adjustments could be made to the detection algorithm to reduce 
	variability with respect to its parameters while also achieving plausible 
	detection results?
\end{description}
